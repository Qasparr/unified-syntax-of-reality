## I. INTRODUCTION: THE CASE THAT WAS MADE

Every war needs a casus belli, and the supposed war between science and
religion got its founding documents in the nineteenth century. In 1874,
John William Draper published *History of the Conflict between Religion
and Science*; in 1896, Andrew Dickson White — first president of Cornell
University — followed with *A History of the Warfare of Science with
Theology in Christendom*. Together they forged what scholars now call the
**Draper–White conflict thesis**: the claim that religion and science
have stood in a state of constant, essential warfare.¹

The thesis was enormously successful — and, the historians report, largely
propaganda. Ronald L. Numbers opens *Galileo Goes to Jail and Other Myths
about Science and Religion* (Harvard University Press, 2009) with the
sentence: "The greatest myth in the history of science and religion holds
that they have been in a state of constant conflict."² Twenty-five
historians contributed chapters; their consensus is that White's and
Draper's accounts are "more propaganda than history."³ The scholarly
correction has a name — John Hedley Brooke's **complexity thesis**: the
real record is far too varied to fit any single slogan.⁴ And Peter
Harrison has pressed deeper, showing in *The Territories of Science and
Religion* (2015) that "science" and "religion" *as we now use the words*
are barely three centuries old — the very categories that could "go to
war" are recent Western inventions.⁵

Mark this well, for it is the whole of the argument in seed: **the
conflict was made into *the case* — the presumed state of affairs — and
it was never the *aim* of either science or religion, nor the *method* of
either.** Warfare is what institutions do when they want territory. It is
not what either discipline does when it wants truth.

## IV. THE MANUFACTURED CASE-STUDY: GALILEO

If the conflict thesis needed a martyr, it manufactured one: Galileo
Galilei, the lone scientist crushed by the Church for telling the truth.
The historians decline the script. What actually happened is more
instructive — and far more useful to this thesis — than the morality tale.

The standard record, in brief: in 1616 the heliocentric teaching was
declared "foolish and absurd in philosophy," and Cardinal Bellarmine
warned Galileo not to hold or defend it. In 1632 Galileo published the
*Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* — with the Church's own
imprimatur. In 1633 he was tried by the Inquisition, found "vehemently
suspect of heresy," and sentenced to house arrest, where he lived out his
years writing. He was not tortured — that belongs to the myth-chapters
Numbers's historians debunk.⁹ He remained a Catholic to the end.

What the morality tale omits is everything that makes the affair
intelligible: that Galileo's chief antagonist turned patron turned
prosecutor was Pope Urban VIII himself, a former friend who took the
*Dialogue*'s fool, Simplicio, as a caricature of his own person and never
pardoned the insult; that the trial
unfolded against the Thirty Years' War, with the Pope's political position
collapsing and a demonstration of authority urgently convenient; that the
charge as formalized was disobedience to an injunction, not the
telescopic evidence, which the Church's own astronomers had largely
confirmed.¹⁰ This was court politics, patronage, personality, and
institutional self-defense — the very "institutional posturing" this
thesis strips away — not *religion* at war with *science*. The Church
funded astronomy; Jesuit observatories mapped the heavens; the calendar
itself was a Church computation.

The conflict thesis took this tangled human event and flattened it into
its Exhibit A. That flattening *is* the manufacture this thesis names:
**the case was made, not found.** The Galileo affair proves the opposite
of what Draper and White claimed — that even the best candidate for the
